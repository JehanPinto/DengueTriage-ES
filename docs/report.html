1. Introduction

About half of the world’s population is now at risk of dengue, and 2024 set a record with 14.6 million reported cases and more than 12,000 deaths [1]. Sri Lanka is among the hardest-hit countries; its largest outbreak, in 2017, produced more than 186,000 reported cases [2].

The difficulty with dengue is timing. The fever lasts two to seven days, and the danger comes after it: around defervescence, usually on days three to seven, capillaries start to leak plasma for 24 to 48 hours, which the guidelines call the critical phase [3]. A patient who feels better as the fever settles can be on the way into shock. In an epidemic, doctors who are not dengue specialists have to decide again and again whether a patient can go home, needs admission, or needs emergency treatment.

DengueTriage-ES makes that decision in the way the WHO guideline sets out. From a patient’s clinical findings, the system:

  • checks whether the dengue case definition is met,
  • classifies the case as dengue without warning signs, dengue with warning signs, or severe dengue,
  • assigns WHO management group A (home care), B (in-hospital care) or C (emergency treatment),
  • states the guideline’s action plan, detects the start of the critical phase and checks the discharge criteria, and
  • explains every conclusion by listing each rule used and the guideline page it came from.

The system is limited to adult patients and assesses one point in time. A finding that was not assessed is treated as absent, and the system has not been clinically validated. It was built for teaching and must not be used to treat patients.

2. System Design

2.1 Knowledge engineering and sources

No domain expert was interviewed; as the brief allows, the knowledge was taken from published clinical guidelines instead. All 35 rules come from the WHO guideline Dengue: Guidelines for Diagnosis, Treatment, Prevention and Control [3], which defines both the case classification and the A/B/C management groups. The Sri Lanka national guideline for adults [4] was read for the local setting; because it uses the older dengue fever / dengue haemorrhagic fever (DF/DHF) terms, the rules follow the WHO classification directly. CDC guidance [5] was used to cross-check wording and thresholds. The process followed is summarised below.

ExplanationCited rule trace, HOW proof trees and WHY answers during questioning

Guideline text was turned into rules using four fixed conventions. A list of alternatives became a class of facts tested with any_of. “Two of the following” became atleast(2, Class). A numeric threshold became a yes/no fact that the clinician answers, which keeps arithmetic out of the rule base entirely. An exclusion such as “without warning signs” became an explicit absent(…) premise. For example, the WHO text states that a patient “is considered to have shock if the pulse pressure … is ≤ 20 mm Hg … or he/she has signs of poor capillary perfusion” [3, p. 28]. That sentence became two rules: R03 IF pulse pressure ≤ 20 mmHg THEN shock, and R04 IF signs of poor capillary perfusion THEN shock.

2.2 Architecture

Everything the clinician sees passes through the console interface, which hands the answers, or a chosen goal, to the inference engine. The engine takes its rules from the knowledge base and writes every confirmed and derived fact to working memory, together with the rule that produced it. The explanation facility builds its traces and proof trees from those stored justifications. The engine itself contains no clinical knowledge, so a different knowledge base could be loaded without changing it.

3.2 Rules

In the code, a rule is the term rule(Id, Layer, Conclusion, Premises), written directly above a source(Id, Document, Page) fact that cites it. A premise can be a fact, absent(Fact), any_of(List) or atleast(N, Class).

Figure 2 shows the decision the rules encode. The order of the checks is the guideline’s: severity comes before warning signs, and warning signs before host factors, so the most urgent group always applies. The guideline does not state in words that Group C overrides Group B, but its ordered triage implies it, so rules R28–R31 each include “NOT severe dengue”. This is the only design choice in the rule base that is not a direct transcription.

4. Inference Engine and Explanation

4.1 Forward chaining (full consultation)

Forward chaining begins with every answer the user gave. It repeatedly looks for a rule whose premises are all satisfied and whose conclusion is not yet known, fires it, and stops when a full pass fires nothing new. The rules are divided into the six layers of Section 3.2, and each layer is completed before the next begins. This is needed because of negation: without layers, R24 (“dengue without warning signs”) could fire before R02 had found a warning sign, and the answer would depend on the order the rules appear in the file. Since every absent(…) premise refers only to a lower layer, the result does not depend on rule order.

Scenario T04 (dengue shock syndrome) shows the layers at work. Layer 1 finds a warning sign and shock (R02, R03); layer 2 establishes probable dengue and severe plasma leakage (R08, R09); layer 3 confirms the dengue case (R17); layer 4 concludes severe dengue (R19); layer 5 classifies it (R22); and layer 6 assigns Group C and adds the NSAID warning (R26, R34).

4.2 Backward chaining (testing one hypothesis)

Here the starting point is a hypothesis chosen from a menu of five, such as “Does this patient have severe dengue?”. The engine looks up every rule whose conclusion matches the hypothesis and tries to prove that rule’s premises in turn, putting a question to the user only when a proof reaches a fact nobody has supplied yet. A counting premise stops as soon as enough members are found. 

Goal: severe dengue            R19: dengue case AND severe plasma leakage
├─ dengue case                 R17 <- R08: endemic area, fever, 2 criteria
│    ask endemic area -> yes;  ask fever -> yes
│    ask nausea/vomiting -> yes;  ask rash -> yes   (2 found; 4 not asked)
└─ severe plasma leakage       R09 <- shock <- R03
     ask pulse pressure <= 20 mmHg -> yes
Result: severe dengue PROVED after 5 questions (forward mode asks 42)

4.3 Explanation

Three facilities share the justifications stored in working memory. At the end of every consultation the system prints a numbered trace of every fired rule in sequence, with its premises (including those satisfied because something was absent), its conclusion, and its guideline section and page. Menu option 5 prints a HOW proof tree for any single conclusion. During backward chaining, typing w at a question shows WHY it is being asked: the chain of goals the engine is trying to prove at that moment. If a goal cannot be proved, the system says that no rule’s conditions were met and that the condition has not been medically ruled out.

5. User Interface

DengueTriage-ES is operated from a console window. A text interface was chosen because the main output, the reasoning trace, is long and indented, and it reads better as text. The main menu (Figure 3) offers both consultation modes, 12 built-in scenarios that run without any typing, the trace, the HOW explanation, a browser for the knowledge base, the test suite and help.

In forward mode the 42 questions appear in eight labelled sections. The user answers y or n (Enter alone means no), and can type s to skip the rest of a section. The result shows the classification, the management group, the guideline action plan and the cited trace (Figure 4).

Figure 5 shows a goal-driven session: questions arrive singly, and typing w at any of them reveals the goal chain behind it.

7. How to Run the System

Code: DengueTriage-ES-code.zip, submitted with this report.

Required software: SWI-Prolog 8.0 or later (tested on 10.0.2) from swi-prolog.org/Download.html, or on Windows run winget install --id SWI-Prolog.SWI-Prolog. Nothing else has to be installed or configured, and the system runs offline.

Steps:

  1. Unzip DengueTriage-ES-code.zip into any folder.
  2. On Windows, double-click run.bat; it finds SWI-Prolog even when it is not on the PATH. On macOS or Linux, run sh run.sh. On any platform, swipl -g start -t halt main.pl also works.
  3. To see a prepared case, type 3, then t04. Type 5 and then 1 to see the proof tree.
  4. For a new patient, choose 1 to answer every question, or 2 to test one hypothesis (type w at any question to see why it is asked).
  5. Type 0 to quit.

To run the tests, double-click test.bat or run swipl -g run_tests -t halt main.pl. Each of the three groups should end “0 failed”, followed by “every rule in the knowledge base carries a source: YES”.

Example input (scenario T02, option 1): answer y to endemic area, fever, nausea or vomiting, aches and pains, abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, tolerates oral fluids and passes urine; press Enter (no) for every other question.

Example output: DENGUE WITH WARNING SIGNS; GROUP B – refer for in-hospital management; the WHO fluid regimen and monitoring plan; six rules (R02, R08, R17, R23, R27, R34), each listed with its source.
